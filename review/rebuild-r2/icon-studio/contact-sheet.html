WIM Icon Studio — contact sheet

Three candidate constructions per glyph on the primitives grammar (48u grid, one 2.5u stroke, round caps and joins, dot terminals, open linework). Each candidate rendered at display size (96), rail size (40), and riser size (22), on black, carrying the unit-warm luminance treatment.

fish

A the vesica

Vesica body from two meeting arcs, nose right; open-chevron tail with dot terminals; one gill arc; stroke-scale eye.

B the ichthys

The carved two-arc fish: both arcs spring from the nose and cross at the tail — the oldest fish mark in the record, redrawn.

C the skeleton

Constellation fish: spine line, rib ticks, head arc, chevron tail — the fish as star-chart bones.

grain

A the kernel arcs

Wheat ear as open arcs: three mirrored kernel strokes sweeping off the stem, each ending in a dot terminal; awn leaning one unit.

B the constellation ear

Dots as grains: a straight stem, short stalk ticks, and six grain dots in mirrored pairs — the ear drawn as stars.

C the bowed stalk

Barley bowing under its own weight: one arced stalk, three awns fanning from the head, two grain dots at the neck.

sandals

A the outsole and thong

One sandal, top view: footprint outsole (fuller outer edge, tapered heel) under the thong V, toe-post dot at the apex.

B the harness

The sandal reduced to its straps: toe post with its dot, the V running back, the heel band closing the loop — no outsole at all.

C the strapped sole

The same footprint outsole crossed by three strap bars instead of the thong — the workaday sandal, banded.

cattle

A the lyre head

Front-on: lyre horns with dot-terminal tips, the head narrowing to the muzzle, nostrils at stroke scale. The silhouette is the animal.

B the bucranium

The ox-skull mark ancient coins actually carried: one wide horn-brow sweep, the hanging muzzle, nostril dots.

C the horn constellation

Brow line, two horn arcs with dot tips, eye dots, chin arc — the head implied entirely by its stars.

salt

A the crystal facet

Halite flat-on: the diamond facet with a single grain dot at its center — no isometric box, no false depth.

B the heap

The measured pile on its ground line, three grain dots falling onto it — salt as the poured, pinched, spent good.

C the crystal cluster

Three halite facets heaped together — one large, two small — the pile drawn as its own crystals.

shells

A the cowrie

The money shell, top view: egg outline, S-curved aperture, four tooth ticks crossing it.

B the aperture

The cowrie reduced to its mouth: two lip arcs, the S between them, tooth ticks, dot terminals — the part that names the shell.

C the scallop fan

Fan construction: rays from a hinge dot to a rim arc — the shell as radiating lines.

iron

A the flat ingot

The cast bar face-on: one trapezoid with a row of three rivet dots — no receding faces, no perspective.

B the iron mark

The alchemical sign for iron redrawn in the grammar: the circle and the angled arrow — the metal named by its oldest symbol.

C the bloom

Raw ore before the cast: an arc-built lump carrying two strike hatches — iron as it comes out of the furnace.

metals

A the stack

The family: three flat ingots of iron’s trapezoid, stacked two-and-one, all face-on.

B the bars

The stack edge-on: three bars narrowing upward, dot terminals at every end — the hoard as constellation.

C the ziggurat

The whole family in one stepped contour: two courses of ingots merged into a single engraved outline.

gold

A the planchet

The unmarked round: a blank planchet with one inner luster arc — deliberately not yet a struck coin.

B the sun mark

The alchemical sign for gold: the circle with its center dot — the round that needs no stamp to be itself.

C the nugget

Native gold before the melt: a faceted lump with two facet lines meeting at a point of light.

coinage

A the incuse square

Gold’s round taking the stamp: the quadratum incusum, the first mint mark in the record.

B the strike

The die landing: four radial ticks and a center dot inside the round — the moment of coining, not its result.

C the beaded rim

The struck coin’s oldest ornament: the round carrying its ring of rim beads — dots as bead-knots.

paper

A the folded note

The note with one corner folded: the fold breaks the app-icon rectangle; the medallion ring sits off-center.

B the deed

The claim as document: upright sheet, two script lines, the issuer’s seal ring below — paper as promise, not as bill.

C the counterfoil

The note torn from its stub: a zigzag tear for a left edge — every claim check implies the ledger it was cut from.

fiat

A the severed tether

The note floating over its own broken anchor line — the claim layer with the cut drawn in, designed for the severance state.

B the drift

The note caught mid-float: the rectangle let go of its grid, skewed as if sliding on air — unanchored by construction.

C the empty seal

Paper’s contents with the paper removed: seal ring and script lines floating with no sheet under them — the note without backing.

bitcoin

A the struck ₿

The symbol redrawn whole in the set’s stroke: stem, two bowls, and the four protruding strokes ending in dot terminals.

B the double strike

The mark reduced to its two through-struck verticals carrying the bowls — the strokes run the full height, dots at all four ends.

C the constellation ₿

Spine and two open bowl arcs, with the four protrusions left as unconnected dots — the symbol implied, star-chart style.

through-time

A the hourglass

Time’s own instrument, flat-on: two open chambers at a waist, grain dots falling through — the good passing intact from now to later.

B the vessel

The granary jar: an open amphora holding three grain dots — value parked on purpose, the store drawn as what a store is.

C the horizon arc

Constellation: a timeline with two moments ticked, one arc carrying a dot from the first to the second — value in flight across time.

between-people

A the exchange arcs

Two person-dots joined by opposing travel arcs, a value dot riding each apex — the discovery slide’s trade routes compressed to a mark.

B the two figures

Two minimal figures — head ring, shoulder arc — facing each other across one value dot; the trade drawn as the people it moves between.

C the facing chevrons

Give and take reduced to geometry: two chevrons pointing at one another, the value dot held at the meeting point.

measure

A the beam balance

The oldest instrument of monetary measure: post, beam, two hanging pans — value weighed, not guessed.

B the graduated rule

The measuring stick of value itself: a baseline with alternating graduations, one good-dot held against the scale.

C the dividers

Drafting dividers spanning a dimension: hinge dot at the apex, two legs, the measured chord drawn between their tips.

collectible

A the strung beads

Three bead dots threaded on a sagging strand — shells and beads, the first goods held for their own strange reasons.

B the kept shelf

A shelf line holding three small treasures of different sizes under a sheltering arc — a few things, kept because somebody cares.

C the cut stone

A faceted gem, flat-on: crown, girdle line, pavilion point — the scarce, interesting object before it is anyone’s money.

palladium

A the Pallas mark

The sign of the asteroid Pallas — lozenge on a crossed stem — the sign palladium was named for in 1803; the glyph carries the beat’s own date.

B the assay ingot

The metals-family trapezoid with an assay scratch instead of iron’s rivets — the metal perpetually tested, never crowned.

C the satellite round

The round family answered: gold’s circle with its center empty, a small dot riding outside the rim — the metal that orbits the throne.

dollar

A the struck $

The universal mark whole, in the set’s stroke: one through-struck stem carrying the S, dot terminals at stem and curve ends.

B the double strike

The older two-stem form, the stems run full height the way the bitcoin candidate’s do — the S carried on a double strike.

C the coin S

The milled round carrying the bare S — the dollar as coin rather than letterform, no stem at all.

peso

A the sol de mayo

The sun from Argentina’s first peso coinage of 1813: an open ring under eight rays, cardinal tips dotted — the coin’s own face, no gold-mark center dot.

B the piece of eight

The peso’s ancestor, the real de a ocho: the numeral eight as two open rounds, struck through once by the assayer’s line.

C the struck P

The abbreviation that named the sign: a P with one horizontal strike, dot terminals — the peso as letterform.

brick

A the coursing

A wall fragment as pure line: three course lines, head joints staggered bond-wise between them — brickwork with no filled shape.

B the floor by floor

The savings building mid-rise: walls, two finished floor lines, and the top floor left open — bought floor by floor as the money comes in.

C the bonded pair

One brick on two — the smallest true bond: three course edges, the middle one running long, joints closing the courses.

gate

A the gateposts

Two upright posts with capped tips, straddling the climb line — a gate stands vertical even on a slope; passage is between them.

B the arch

A single arch bridging the line — the stage passed under, footed with dot terminals.

C the climb chevron

One chevron pointing up the ladder, apex dotted — the next stage unlocked, direction drawn as the mark itself.

tie

A the plumb drop

A vertical tether ending in its bob — the upper layer hanging its weight on the one below.

B the counterfoil jog

The tether with the counterfoil’s tear drawn into it: a Z-jog mid-line — the link that is a redeemable slip, in the paper family’s language.

C the hook and eye

A J-hook reaching down from the upper layer toward the eye-dot beneath — the layer holds the base, it does not stand on its own.

real-estate

A the gable

The building as its own silhouette: two roof strokes meeting at an apex over the walls, a ground line running past both, one door aperture. Flat-on, eaves dotted.

B the elevation

A facade drawn flat: outer walls, one floor line, four window ticks in two courses, ground line extending past the walls — the asset, not the home.

C the plot

Title to land seen from above: the boundary with a corner dot at each turn, the footprint set inside it — ownership of ground rather than of a house.

shares

A the parted round

The whole with one part taken out of it and set beside it, on its own radius, the same size as the gap it left — a share is a fraction of an enterprise, and the fraction is what you hold.

B the divided round

The whole still whole: a ring with three radii from a center dot, the held sector marked by dot terminals where its boundaries meet the rim.

C the stacked slips

A bundle of certificates: three offset leaves with one ruled entry line on the front slip — the holding as a stack of paper.

ledger

A the ruled book

The account book open on its spine: two page arcs springing from one center line, two ruled entries on each page — the record, not the money.

B the tally column

One account column: the rule that opens it, three entry lines shortening down the page, dot terminals where each entry stops.

C the double entry

Bookkeeping’s own construction: two columns on one baseline, divided by the central rule, one entry dot in each — every credit answered by a debit.

operation

A the steadying pulse

One line, read left to right: two irregular beats of differing height and spacing, then three even ones at one amplitude on one baseline. The value delivered is the resolution — the mark states the outcome, not the procedure, and needs no knowledge of surgery to read.

B the mending arc

The alternative direction the round was set against: a break in a curve, closed. Two arc segments approach from either side and a third bridges the gap between their inner terminals — repair drawn as continuity restored, with the join dotted.

C the settled interval

The same argument as A without a waveform: four vertical strokes whose spacing and height are erratic on the left and regular on the right, over one baseline. Rhythm alone, no medical vocabulary at all.

shoe

A the profile last

A shoe from the side, built from the three features that make one legible: the flat sole, the ankle opening dipped into the top line, and the toe spring lifting off the ground. Two lace ticks cross the instep. Distinct from Section 2's sandal, which is an outsole-and-thong mark.

B the head-on last

The shoe seen head-on: the ankle opening as an open arc, the upper falling to a rounded toe box, three lace rungs across it, the sole a bar beneath. Symmetry does the work the profile's silhouette could not.

C the print

The impression rather than the object: sole outline with the ball and heel separated, three toe dots.

meal

A the cut and the bone

The steak itself: one closed-contour cut with the bone drawn as an open hook at its edge and a single marbling stroke inside. Reads as food, not as a plate of something.

B the plate and cover

The served meal: dome arc with its handle dot over a plate line, the oldest sign for a meal about to be eaten.

C the fork and the cut

Utensil and food together: three-tine fork left, the cut as an open arc right — the moment of eating rather than the object.

wine

A the glass

The stemmed glass in three strokes: bowl arc, stem, foot — the most legible wine mark at riser scale, and the one that reads as a good rather than as a container of liquid.

B the bottle

Neck, shoulder and body as one open contour with a punt line at the base and a shoulder label tick.

C the amphora

The ancient vessel: two open handles off a tapering body, rhyming with Section 2's traded goods rather than with a modern bottle.
