# 1. Purpose and delivery context

This deck's goal is to be the best presentation and explanation of "What is Money?" ever created. The original version was delivered live at a Bitcoin Bush Bash in Byron Bay and received exceptionally well; this rebuild produces the **canonical version**: a recorded, screenshared delivery published on YouTube, built with **no time constraint** — the only criterion for any element is that it deserves to exist.

Consequences of the recorded format:

- The presenter addresses **"you," the viewer** — direct address works; "everyone in this room" phrasing does not. Rhetorical questions and deliberate silences work on video and are part of the design.
- Pacing is authored, not improvised: holds, pauses, and black frames are specified per beat because the recording preserves them exactly.
- Live deliveries may happen again later; they are cuts of the canonical version, never the other way around.

**Audience priority when needs conflict:** 1) intelligent newcomers / pre-coiners (zero prerequisites assumed), 2) — very close behind — Bitcoin OGs, 3) Austrian economists and skeptical academics. Operating principle: the explanation that most impresses an OG is a *perfect explanation of the fundamentals* (the 3Blue1Brown effect), so designing for the intelligent newcomer at the highest craft level serves both audiences at once.

**Master design principle (the presenter's own words, adopted as law):** do not hand the audience the conclusion that Bitcoin fits anywhere — give them the knowledge to come to that conclusion themselves.

# 3. The Narrative Constitution

Every rule below carries its rationale. Future sessions inherit the *why*, not just the *what*. Violating these is a defect regardless of any other instruction.

## 3.1 Emergence rules

- **Never describe Bitcoin as "engineered," "purpose-built," "designed as a solution," or "repurposed."** Grounding: the deck's central historical claim (D2 below) is that money is *discovered by markets, not designed by anyone*. Calling Bitcoin engineered-to-be-money would exempt Bitcoin from the very law the deck spends twenty minutes establishing. It is also the historically weaker claim: a cash system was designed; the store-of-value role *emerged* through adoption — the better story and the true one.
- Money is presented as an emergent market phenomenon. The state's real historical role is conceded honestly (see beat 2.3's canonical sentence) — concession of the true part is what disarms the objection.

## 3.2 Neutrality rules

- **Before 4.01, Bitcoin may be *described*, never *argued*.** Facts in the neutral register, at least one honest limitation in the same breath, zero adjectives of advocacy. Bitcoin's two descriptive appearances are 2.8 and 3.7, both specified below.
- **The evidence rule:** facts about *the money itself* (purchasing power of a currency, supply data, discovery dates, prices) may appear on screen. Evidence about *social outcomes* with contested causal chains (wealth gaps, inequality since 1971) stays in spoken word and Q&A only. Rationale: the former survives any skeptic; the latter invites a fight the framework hasn't yet earned the right to win.
- **The generalized honesty rule (F-06 generalized):** every monetary good presented on stage gets at least one stated weakness — fiat included, Bitcoin included. An unexplained strength reads as advocacy; an explained weakness reads as rigor.
- No political sorting: no villain narratives, no country singled out for monetary failure without the structural framing specified in the relevant beat, no partisan-coded evidence.

## 3.3 Revelation-protection rules

- **The claim ladder:** the word "claim" appears exactly three times as a concept, one rung per act. Rung 1 (beat 2.6): a banknote as "a claim on gold in a vault" — plain historical usage, planted casually. Rung 2 (beat 3.6): bank deposits as claims on base money — and the section ends on the unanswered question of what the base itself is a claim on. Rung 3 (4.04, existing): the generalization — money itself is an earned, transferable claim on value. Rungs 1 and 2 must never anticipate the generalization; 4.04 must land as *recognition*, not instruction.
- **No property checklist before Section 4.** Section 2 shows *that* properties decide the competition (via named single-dimension failures); Section 3 gives functions and stages. The derivation of the ideal property list is 4.10–4.14's revelation and must stay virgin.
- **The one-term rule:** each of Sections 1–3 may introduce at most one technical term. Section 2 introduces **salability** (its single allowed term, introduced once in 2.2, used thereafter). Sections 1 and 3 introduce none. "Schelling point" is explicitly excluded (concept present in 2.3 without the label; may be name-dropped in spoken word only).
- The claim/carrier vocabulary, the 100-Year Test, and the inversion method never appear before Section 4.

## 3.4 Table and Section 4 integrity (inherited, re-affirmed)

- No total score, no winner badge, no Bitcoin-column highlight; "Don't trust the table. Verify every score." stays.
- All fifty scores stay as they are (incl. Bitcoin 5/5 durability with its notes defense, 2/5 track record, 4/5 fungibility).
- Row header **PROPERTY**; real-estate asset labeled **REAL ESTATE**.
- "The monetary competition is decided at the margin" remains spoken-only.
- 4.23 is the true final frame; the close is a silent "Thank you." with the disclaimer in its notes.
- Boyapati is attributed on stage when the monetization stages appear (beat 3.3). Florian Bruce-Boye's attribution (existing) is untouched.

## 3.5 Language and craft rules

- American English throughout, all user-visible text and notes ("labor," "monetization," "judgment," "jewelry"). Typographic apostrophes and quotes in visible copy.
- Neutral register: no dramatizing adjectives in factual statements (the "violently volatile" class of error). State the fact; let it be heavy on its own.
- Each contender/example dies of exactly **one clean wound** — one failure, one dimension, no pile-ons.
- Canonical wording fragments in this brief marked **[canonical]** are approved direction: implement them verbatim in placeholder copy/notes at the specified register (on-screen vs spoken). The presenter finalizes all wording in his own notes pass; visible copy changes after that pass require his sign-off.
- **The pacing rule: one advance per spoken beat.** Any text element the presenter speaks over gets its own build, advanced manually. Auto-timed staging is reserved for motion *within* a single visual gesture (a field filling, a collapse, a sweep) — never for sequential lines of copy.
- **Black is a beat, not a seam.** Darkness appears only where the design calls for it — the cold open, an authored clear-to-black, a scripted black build; navigation never manufactures it. Between slides with no shared scene group the transition is a ~300ms crossfade (outgoing dissolves as incoming rises, no black frame between); reduced motion cuts instantly. *(R2.2 §B.)*

# 4. The deposit framework: D1–D9

Section 4's argument presupposes nine deposits in the audience's head. **Every beat in Sections 1–3 must trace to at least one deposit; any element tracing to none is deleted, however good.**

- **D1 — The audience owns the question personally.** They know they're deciding where a life's work gets stored, before any framework appears. *(Section 1.)*
- **D2 — Money is an emergent market phenomenon.** Goods become money by winning a competition; no decree fixes the winner. *(2.1–2.3.)*
- **D3 — Properties decide the competition.** The single most load-bearing deposit: history told as evidence for this law, so 4.10–4.16's method feels obviously correct. *(2.4–2.8.)*
- **D4 — Every money so far has failed or been displaced — and the record includes today's.** Humility, the difficulty that legitimizes the 100-Year Test, the ground for 4.08–4.09, and the fiat purchasing-power evidence deposited where 4.17 can harvest it. *(2.5–2.8, evidence at 2.7.)*
- **D5 — Functions: what they are, that they separate, and why store of value is the entry point.** SoV-first is the analytically correct starting point (Boyapati's order), not the convenient one. *(3.1–3.3.)*
- **D6 — Monetization is staged; young monetary goods are volatile and illiquid by stage, not by verdict.** *(3.4.)*
- **D7 — Incumbency logic: only categorical superiority has ever displaced a monetary incumbent.** The bar Section 4's comparison holds every candidate to. *(3.5.)*
- **D8 — Minimal neutral Bitcoin literacy.** Split: 2.8 delivers three facts + one limitation; 3.7 completes with the 21M cap, direct no-counterparty holding, and stage placement. *(2.8 + 3.7.)*
- **D9 — The claim intuition seeded, never named.** The opening's hours→money conversion plus the claim ladder's first two rungs make 4.04 recognition. *(1.1–1.2, 2.6, 3.6.)*

**Anti-list** (Sections 1–3 must not): pre-argue Bitcoin; use claim/carrier vocabulary beyond the specified ladder rungs; run property scoring; state barter-origin as unqualified historical chronology (framed as the logical problem, standard economic account, per 2.1); front-load inflation grievance (the C-material lives inside D4's historical record at 2.7).

# 5. Beat map — Section 1: The Question (5 beats)

Section 1's entire job: D1 + D9-seed + the promise. Lean is the point.

**1.1 — Cold open: 80,000 hours.**
No title slide first; darkness, then a field of small identical units filling the frame — one per working hour of a human life — count ticking up to ~80,000. One line on screen: *"This is how many hours of your life you will spend working."* **Deliberate visual rhyme:** this unit-field and 4.22's fixed-supply unit-field are the same visual grammar at opposite ends of the argument — a life poured in, the container it's poured into. Never pointed out explicitly. *Creates: what do all these hours become?*

**1.2 — The conversion.**
The hours-field flows, compresses, becomes money — an abstract neutral token (no logos, no bitcoin symbol; neutrality is sacred here). Essence: everything you earn is time changing form; money is where a life's work accumulates. D9's seed, planted in thirty seconds. *Creates: then what exactly IS this stuff?*

**1.3 — The question nobody can answer.**
Direct address to the viewer: *"Without saying what it does — can you say what money is?"* A genuine held pause (recorded silence is a design element). Then the water: one side of nearly every trade on Earth; the most used good in human civilization; touched daily by every person alive — and not one hour of school, ever, on what it actually is. **The title lands here — WHAT IS MONEY? — as the punchline of the silence,** not as a label on slide one. *Creates: why does nobody know this — and does it matter?*

**1.4 — The stakes.**
The mercy line, essentially alone on screen, full black — at most the hours-field from 1.1 faintly present behind it, unchanged, exposed. **[canonical, on-screen]** *"If you don't understand the thing your life's work is stored in, you are at the mercy of those who do."* No historical image, no chart, no montage — restraint is the image (ruling: the wealth-gap-since-1971 genre is excluded here by the evidence rule and by neutrality; the stakes are about the asymmetry of understanding, not the outcomes of debasement). Spoken support carries the asymmetry point briefly, without grievance. *Creates: what would understanding it actually give me?*

**1.5 — The promise and the map.**
How do you understand anything mysterious? Ask where it came from, how it changed, what it must do, and how you'd judge any candidate trying to be it. That sentence *is* the deck's architecture — origin and evolution (Section 2), functions and monetization (Section 3), the test (Section 4) — delivered as method, not agenda slide. Close with the covenant: **[canonical, spoken]** *"By the end you'll have a framework — not my conclusions, a framework you can check for yourself."* ("Don't trust the table" begins in minute three.) A light educational-purposes line lives quietly in this beat's notes; the full disclaimer stays at the close. *Creates → Section 2: where does money actually come from?*

# 6. Beat map — Section 2: Where Money Comes From (8 beats)

Inherits: *"Where does money actually come from?"* Deep structure: **first half = money being born (2.1–2.4); second half = the Evolution Rail as the competition record (2.5–2.8).**

**2.1 — The world without it.**
Strip money away: two people, HAS and WANTS (legacy 2.03/2.04 concept survives, compressed to one beat). Then the wall, made concrete with a triad: the fisherman wants sandals, the sandal-maker wants grain, the farmer wants fish — every pair fails. The coincidence of wants, *felt* rather than defined. **Framing rule (notes):** this is the logical problem every trading society hits — **[canonical, spoken]** *"wherever exchange happens without money, this wall appears"* — not a chronological claim that pure barter economies preceded money everywhere (this framing is the anthropology-proof formulation; keep Menger's logic, drop the contestable chronology). *Creates: how does anyone get over the wall?*

**2.2 — The discovery.**
Someone accepts a good they don't want — *to trade it away later*. The birth of money: not invented, **discovered**. It creates the question every trader must now ask: which good will others most reliably accept? Introduce Section 2's single allowed term: **salability**. *Creates: which goods win that contest?*

**2.3 — The convergence (the murmuration, re-staged).**
The existing murmuration is retained as the base and **re-staged in two phases**: phase one, the pure flock — thousands of independent agents, no leader, coherent motion; phase two, the flock's motion gradually settles, organizing around a single bright point that resolves into the winning good. The viewer watches decentralized choice *converge*, not just swirl. Reduced-motion static substitute retained. D2 lands explicitly: money is an emergent order, like language — nobody invented English, nobody's permission was needed. The honest concession that replaces the legacy overclaim: **[canonical, on-screen or spoken]** *"Governments have stamped, standardized, and monopolized money throughout history — but the phenomenon itself is older than any state and has never needed one to exist."* *Creates: converge on WHAT — what makes one good more salable than another?*

**2.4 — The law of the competition.**
D3's home. Contenders across cultures, each dying of exactly one clean wound, **each wound a different dimension**: cattle — cannot be divided; salt — dissolves in a rainstorm; shells — *"scarce only until someone reaches the right beach — their supply was one ship away from collapse"* **[canonical, register per design]** (supply vulnerability; deliberate setup paid off in 2.5); iron — rusts, and anyone with a furnace can make more. The audience intuits a multi-dimensional competition without ever seeing a checklist. The law: **[canonical, spoken]** *"The market keeps re-running one experiment and keeps selecting for the same thing: the good that survives across space, across time, across scale."* *Creates: so who won — and what happened to the winners?*

**2.5 — The Rail, part I: the metals.**
The Evolution Rail enters as the spine of the section's second half — the competition record made visible. Commodity monies fall to their trade-offs, each defeat *named as a property failure* — the aggry beads destroyed when industrial shipping made them cheap to supply: the historical receipt for 2.4's shell setup, a supply-property defeat with real victims. Metals win on multiple axes; gold and silver win among metals. **Included:** the periodic-table elimination bit — gases escape, reactive metals corrode, radioactive elements kill you; chemistry leaves two candidates — executed as one fast, beautiful build. *Creates: gold won everything — so why isn't there gold in your pocket?*

**2.6 — The Rail, part II: the abstraction ladder.**
Gold's own trade-offs (heavy, hard to verify, dangerous to move) get solved by *layers*: coinage solves verification and division; paper solves portability — a note redeemable in gold, **"a claim on gold in a vault"** (claim ladder rung 1 — plain historical usage, planted casually, never elaborated). Each rung up buys convenience and **pays for it in trust**: the coin trusts the mint; the note trusts the vault. The section's second law. *Creates: what happens when the trust is stretched?*

**2.7 — The Rail, part III: the severance.**
1971 told as dated fact, no villain music: redemption ends; for the first time in the record, the world's money is *pure decree* — the trust rung with nothing under it. Then D4's evidence payload: **the plural-currency purchasing-power chart** — the measured record of the incumbent *on the store-of-value function specifically*: USD, GBP, JPY, and euro-area predecessors, all on one decay axis. Plural is the point: one currency declining reads as "one government mismanaged"; every major currency declining reads as a **structural property of the design** — and no country is singled out. Exact series and figures sourced at implementation (`SOURCES.md` entry required; evidence-about-the-money only). Balanced in the same beat by fiat's genuine achievement, stated plainly: the most universally accepted medium of exchange in history, unmatched payment convenience. The precise residue the viewer should leave with: **the reigning money is extraordinary at moving value and measurably poor at storing it.** (D5(c) pre-loaded; the exact wound Section 4 examines.) *Creates: is this just how it ends — is the record over?*

**2.8 — The Rail, part IV: the pattern and the `?`.**
Camera pulls back — the full rail in one frame for the first time. The section's thesis, earned: **[canonical, staged across advances]** *"No money in this record held the role forever. In the free competition, monies fell when something categorically better arrived. The last incumbent didn't fall that way — it was captured: its custody centralized, its claims over-issued, its redemption cancelled. Either way, the role moves. And the record has no reason to be finished."* (Note — rationale for the two-chapter thesis: the gold→fiat transition was layering-by-choice followed by capture-by-force, not competitive displacement; the two-chapter framing is the historically accurate one and sharpens Section 4's question.) The current system is the youngest money on the rail (~55 years in pure form). The rail extends into dark; the `?` appears (protected element, retained); one new entrant at its edge, in the deck's most neutral register: in 2009, something new — digital, issued by no state and no company, supply fixed by its own rules. Three facts, one honest limitation in the same breath: **[canonical, register per design]** *"It is very young, its price still swings far more than the monies it would compete with, and it is seventeen years into a hundred-year question."* (Neutral phrasing ruling: no dramatizing adjectives; comparison class is *monies*, where the volatility statement is true and calm — and it quietly plants the 2/5 track-record honesty.) Section 2 **refuses to judge** whether it belongs on the rail: we don't yet have the tools. *Creates → Section 3: to judge any candidate, we need to know precisely what a money must DO.*

# 7. Beat map — Section 3: What Money Must Do (7 beats)

Inherits: *"What must a money actually DO?"* Job: D5, D6, D7, D8-completion, the credit beat, and the handoff that makes 4.01 inevitable.

**3.1 — The three functions.**
The triad, cleanly: money moves value **through time** (store of value), **between people** (medium of exchange), and **measures it** (unit of account). Continuity move that lifts it above textbook: these map onto 2.4's competition dimensions — survives across *time* → SoV; across *space* → MoE; across *scale* → UoA. The functions aren't a new framework; they're what winning Section 2's competition looks like from the inside. *Creates: is that a package deal — must one good do all three?*

**3.2 — The functions can separate.**
The insight most talks skip: history is full of economies where people **price in one thing, transact in another, and save in a third**. On-stage example: **Argentina** — apartments priced and sold in dollars for decades, daily payments in pesos, savings in dollars and bricks — with the built-in depoliticizer stated: this pattern persisted *across administrations of every political stripe* for half a century; it's not a story about one government, it's what people do when one function of their money breaks. The principle: **[canonical]** *the functions are separable across goods and across time — which means a good can be money in one function before (or without) the others.* Load-bearing twice later (3.6's scoping and 3.7's stage placement). *Creates: if they can separate, is there an order — which function comes first?*

**3.3 — The order of monetization.**
The section's analytical heart, **attributed on stage: this framing follows Vijay Boyapati.** Collectible → store of value → medium of exchange → unit of account — and the order is *logic*, not accident: nobody accepts a good as **payment** unless they expect it to **hold value** until they spend it; nobody writes **contracts** in a unit nobody **accepts**. Each function gates the next — store of value is the **foundation function**. Quantifier softened: "monetary goods have historically monetized in this pattern," not a law of nature. **This beat is what makes Section 4 rigorous instead of convenient:** analysis starts at the foundation because it must, not because any candidate happens to be good at it. *Creates: what does a good look like while it's still climbing?*

**3.4 — Stage signatures.**
Short and surgical (D6): early-stage monetary goods share a *signature* — small holder base, thin markets, reflexive pricing — so **volatility and illiquidity are properties of the stage, not verdicts on the good**. Gold spent millennia as a collectible curiosity before it was anyone's savings. **Bitcoin is not mentioned.** The viewer files the principle away clean (the inoculation that lets 4.18 argue volatility honestly). *Creates: if properties and functions decide everything, why does the champion never change — why hasn't gold been replaced by something even scarcer?*

**3.5 — The palladium beat.**
D7, the section's freshest material. Three moves, one beat:
1. **The hook fact:** palladium — rarer than gold, genuinely useful, at times *more expensive* — and it never monetized. Not even close.
2. **The timing/network point:** discovered in 1803, arriving to face a monetary network thousands of years old — and a monetary good's value *is* other people's acceptance; latecomers start at zero.
3. **The bar:** palladium offered no *categorical* advantage — and marginal-or-worse has never crossed the moat. **[canonical, spoken]** *"For anything to earn a place on the rail, marginally better is structurally insufficient; only a categorical difference on the deciding properties has ever moved the crown."*
**Logical-integrity rule for this beat:** the concentration and industrial-demand findings (≈80% of mined supply from two countries; demand driven by non-monetary factors) appear in **notes and Q&A only**, framed as *why palladium was not categorical* — never on stage as a parallel explanation, where they would compete with the network argument and hand a skeptic "it just failed because it was worse." One minimal chart on stage: rarity and price versus gold, nothing else (evidence-about-the-good; `SOURCES.md` entry). Look back at the rail: every transition that happened was categorical. The beat silently answers "why not silver / platinum / a new commodity" for the rest of the deck and sets the hurdle 4.16 holds every candidate to — Bitcoin included. *Creates: wait — is the thing in your bank account even the base good we've been talking about?*

**3.6 — What your money actually is.**
The underexplained beat; completes the "this is water" arc from 1.3. The numbers in your bank account are not base money — they are **claims on money**: your bank's IOU, redeemable on demand in base money (cash if you withdraw; central-bank reserves when banks settle). Not metaphor — mechanism: it is why bank runs exist (more claims issued than base held) and why deposits can fail while cash in hand doesn't. Claim ladder rung 2. Then the layer principle, both honest directions: **[canonical]** *"Every mature money has grown credit layers on top of itself — notes on gold, deposits on notes, payment apps on deposits. Layers are not a scam — they are how money scales to daily commerce, and they've existed as long as sound money has. But layers inherit the soundness of their base — a tower is only as good as its foundation."* The scoping move the whole deck needs: this presentation's question is not about payment apps or banking layers — it is about **the foundation asset underneath them all** (pre-scopes the "can't buy coffee" objection class as a layer question, before Bitcoin is ever argued). On stage: one clean layer diagram, one tight beat (expansion to two beats only if implementation proves it cramped). Bills of exchange, acceptance houses, clearing history: spoken word and Q&A only. **The beat ends on the unanswered question, held:** **[canonical, spoken, unanswered]** *"Every layer is a claim on the layer below. So what is the bottom layer a claim on?"* — planted twenty minutes before 4.04 answers it. *Creates: so what should the foundation be?*

**3.7 — Where Bitcoin is.**
D8 completed, same neutral register as 2.8. Placement on the monetization map as description: collectible in its first years; now visibly in the **store-of-value stage** — held by individuals, funds, and lately institutions and states (adoption stated as fact, zero celebration); not medium of exchange or unit of account at scale. Literacy completed: the supply cap — 21 million units, fixed by the protocol's rules; and the property that it can be held **directly, with no counterparty**, like a bearer asset (the fact 4.15's holding assumptions quietly depend on). The honesty line, same breath: it is early in a process that history shows can stall — candidates have died mid-monetization before. Then the section turns and faces forward — the `?` returns, and the viewer now holds functions, an order, stage signatures, and the height of the bar — everything except a rigorous way to run the judgment. The handoff: **[canonical, spoken]** *"So how do you tell — for this candidate or any other — whether it deserves the foundation role? Not by anyone's opinion. By a judgment you can build from first principles."* The skeptic's opening becomes the deck's question; Section 4 is the answer walking through the door it opened. *Creates → 4.01: the thought experiment.*

## 9.2 Notes (script standard)

Speaker notes are the **verbatim recording script**: the exact words the presenter reads aloud while screen-recording the deck, slide by slide. They are not slide explainers, design rationale, or delivery instructions.

Format:
- Flowing spoken prose in the presenter's voice, first person, direct address to the viewer.
- **`[→]`** marks every advance (arrow press). The words after a `[→]` are spoken over the build it triggers. Every build of the slide appears as exactly one `[→]` in its notes, in order. The advance that *leaves* the slide belongs to the next slide's script, not this one.
- *Italic bracketed stage directions* are allowed sparingly and only where silence or timing is itself the content — e.g. *[hold — let the silence sit]*. No other meta-commentary.
- Canonical spoken lines from this brief appear verbatim at their exact positions.
- American English, typographic apostrophes, no hard-wrapped lines, no wrapping quotation marks.
- The presenter rewrites and finalizes every script in his own voice at the end of the rebuild (after R6); until then, scripts are drafted to be readable as-is.

Sourcing bases for on-screen figures live in `docs/SOURCES.md` only — never inside the script.

## 9.3 The wayfinding device and the color arc

Formal section openers (including all AI-painting imagery) are removed. Section boundaries **from S2→S3 onward** are marked by the **waypoint return**: a brief interstitial in which the method line from 1.5 returns — completed waypoints settled and dim, the current section's waypoint igniting — then dissolves into the section's first beat. At the S1→S2 boundary the device does not return: the first ignition lives inside 1.5 itself, as its final build (the device's value is the line *returning after absence*; four seconds after its introduction there is no absence to return from). Choreography per section is specified in that section's session brief.

The deck's accent (orange) enters for the first time at the ignition of the first waypoint, is used sparingly and structurally through Sections 2–3 (waypoint ignitions, rail emphasis, singular argumentative highlights), and reaches full deployment in Section 4 where it already lives. The accent is never decorative.

## 9.4 The compositional standard (deck-wide)

What makes this deck's frames read as one hand:

1. **One idea per frame.** Each build lands at most one new text element. On-screen text is the distillation — a line, not a paragraph; the script carries everything else. When a new line lands, prior lines dim or clear unless they are being deliberately accumulated (a list being built is one accumulating element).
2. **The two-element budget.** Outside structural diagrams, at most two text elements are fully bright at once (e.g., a kicker-scale label plus one statement). Sparse labels on diagrams don't count against the budget; paragraphs on slides are forbidden.
3. **Line-language visuals.** Nodes, thin strokes, dot terminals, grammar glyphs, luminous discs, canvas fields. No photography, no AI-generated imagery, no filled panels except structural slabs (the tower), no icon-library stock.
4. **Negative space is a material.** Centered or mirror-symmetric compositions; generous emptiness; nothing touches frame edges.
5. **Quiet chrome.** No persistent per-slide header convention. Kickers are rare, small, and earned (an attribution, a frame that must be named). The deck's structure is carried by the waypoint device, not by headers.
6. **Orange is meaning.** The accent marks argument — ignitions, foundations, the claim, emphasis dots — never decoration, never chrome.
7. **Black is a beat, not a seam.** (Restated from §3.5.)
8. **Structural diagrams are exempt from the text budget but not from the hand:** the comparison table, the rail, the tower, the ladder follow their own established patterns — thin strokes, grammar glyphs, consistent rhythm.
9. **The two registers.** The deck speaks one language in two registers, and every visual is assigned to exactly one:
   - **The line grammar** (rules 1–8): for everything *structural* — mechanisms, records, comparisons, diagrams, labels. Where abstraction is the argument, abstraction is the form.
   - **The dark-field register**: photographic/rendered imagery, permitted only where *sensory concreteness is the argument* — a human being's hour, a meal, a good someone's savings lived in. Its grade is fixed: pure black ground (no environment, no horizon), a single warm key light (≈2700–3200K), the subject emerging from darkness with soft falloff, no text baked in, one consistent look across every image as if from a single shoot. Outliers are regenerated, never grandfathered.
   - **Governance:** dark-field never enters a diagram; glyphs never carry a sensory beat at display scale. Register switches are designed moments, not convenience. Every slide's register assignment is recorded in the decision log. Each recurring subject may exist in both forms — the dark-field render for its sensory introduction, the grammar glyph for its structural life — and a designed handoff may transform one into the other.
10. **The brightness floors.** Rule 1 says prior lines dim. These two say how far, and what may never be dimmed:
    - **The legibility floor.** No element that has landed and is then dimmed — a dimmed prior, a `defeated` stop, a settled block — falls below **55% of the brightness it landed at**. (For anything that lands at the deck's full text brightness, that is ~0.55 alpha on black.) Dimming means *recede*; it never means *vanish into murk*. A frame whose every element sits at a third of full voice has no hierarchy — it is uniformly faint, which reads as an unfinished render rather than as emphasis.
    - **The last-lit invariant.** The most recently landed text element of any build renders at **full brightness** — its own maximum across the slide. Dimming applies only to elements that came before it. A build that lands a statement and dims it in the same breath has nothing at full voice, and the frame no longer says which sentence is being spoken. (2.8's thesis lines are the reference case: each dims one step as the next lands, and the closing line holds full brightness as the frame's end state.)
    - **One exception, and it is a handoff rather than a dim:** an entire scene layer that has given the frame to a superseding scene may recede past the floor, because nothing on it is being read. The rail beneath the risen periodic table (2.5) is the deck's single instance; it is recorded in the decision log rather than granted by the rule.
