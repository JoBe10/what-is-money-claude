# 1. Executive summary

The presentation makes the case for Bitcoin by first asking a more fundamental question:

> **What is money, how did it evolve, what functions does it perform, and what does a store of value actually need to carry through time?**

It does not begin with blockchain mechanics, price predictions, trading, cryptocurrency markets or a list of Bitcoin features without context.

The deck moves through four major intellectual movements:

1. **Opening and orientation:** Bitcoin is important but cannot be understood in isolation. The audience receives a path through money rather than a technical lecture.
2. **Evolution of money:** Monetary forms emerge and compete because societies face recurring trade-offs involving scarcity, salability, portability, divisibility, verification, trust and control.
3. **Functions of money:** Medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value are separated. Bitcoin is positioned most credibly today as an emerging store of value.
4. **The ideal store of value:** The presentation derives the investment thesis from first principles rather than asserting it.

Section 4 begins with a surgeon who performs a specialised service and receives money. He has delivered value, but has not yet selected the final goods and services he wants in return. Money is introduced as:

> **an earned, transferable claim on value**

Spending redeems the claim. Saving keeps the exchange open. The claim requires a carrier. A store of value must preserve the claim’s purchasing power and redeemability through time.

The 100-Year Test sends the claim from 2026 to 2126. Inversion asks how the carrier could fail. Ten failure modes produce ten requirements. Gold, fiat, Bitcoin, real estate and shares are then compared transparently.

The final case for Bitcoin does not claim Bitcoin must replace all other assets. It explains that when the dominant currency is not trusted to preserve purchasing power over long periods, savings demand migrates into other assets and adds **monetary premium**. Bitcoin competes for that premium, not for the full productive, consumptive or aesthetic value of those assets.

The existing stock of global wealth need not move at once. Every new saving, inheritance, refinancing, asset sale, maturity and portfolio rebalance creates another marginal decision about which carrier to use. Against a supply that cannot expand through discretionary issuance, marginal flows can reprice the outstanding stock.

The presentation lands on:

> **Bitcoin does not need to replace everything.**  
> **It only needs to become the preferred place to store the next unit of value.**

# 2. Core thesis and boundaries

The complete causal chain is:

> **Human beings need to exchange value → direct exchange does not scale → monetary goods emerge → monetary forms compete on their properties and dependencies → money performs several functions → an emerging money normally develops store-of-value demand before becoming a dominant medium of exchange and unit of account → Bitcoin is best assessed first as an emerging store of value → a store of value is a carrier for an unredeemed monetary claim → carriers can fail in identifiable ways → those failures imply requirements → Bitcoin appears structurally strong against those requirements → when fiat is not trusted for long-term purchasing-power preservation, savings demand migrates into other assets → that creates monetary premium → Bitcoin competes for that monetary demand → repeated marginal allocation decisions can matter greatly when supply cannot respond.**

The deck does **not** claim that:

- Bitcoin is attractive at every price;
- Bitcoin must appreciate in any particular period;
- Bitcoin is risk-free or dependency-free;
- Bitcoin will replace property, shares, gold or fiat;
- the comparison table is objectively incontestable;
- all wealth will migrate to Bitcoin;
- the combined market capitalisation of other assets is Bitcoin’s automatic addressable market;
- fixed supply creates a mechanical price formula.

The thesis is about structural monetary competition, not guaranteed return.

# 4. Why the arc is structured this way

## Relevance before abstraction

The audience first needs a reason to care. Beginning with dense monetary theory would feel academic; beginning with blockchain would feel technical; beginning with price would feel promotional.

## History before judgment

Without monetary history, Bitcoin’s features look arbitrary. History makes scarcity, portability, divisibility, verification and resistance to control intelligible as responses to recurring problems.

## Functions before the store-of-value thesis

A viewer may object that Bitcoin is not money because it is not yet universally used for everyday payments. Separating the functions of money and introducing stages of monetisation makes the store-of-value focus a logical sequencing argument rather than an evasion.

## Define the job before listing properties

The rebuilt Section 4 refuses to begin with “What properties would an ideal store of value have?” It first asks what the store-of-value function is actually carrying. This makes the later framework feel earned rather than imported.

## Concrete example before abstract definition

The surgeon grounds the argument in a recognisable exchange. The abstraction emerges from the example rather than arriving as a philosophical lecture.

## Claim before carrier

Separating the monetary function from the asset embodying it is the conceptual hinge of the deck. It supports the historical lineage, the store-of-value definition, the 100-Year Test and the candidate comparison.

## Inversion before the property list

Failure is easier to imagine than perfection. Asking how the carrier could fail produces concrete requirements and removes the recognisable “design the perfect store of value” thought experiment from the old version.

## Transparent comparison before investment conclusion

The audience sees the framework and assumptions before seeing the final investment thesis. The table has no total, winner badge or Bitcoin-column highlight. The viewer is explicitly invited to challenge every score.

## Monetary premium before marginal flows

Bitcoin does not compete with shelter, profits or aesthetic value. The deck first isolates the monetary component of asset demand, then explains how recurring marginal allocation decisions can shift that demand.

## Distilled ending

The final slide deliberately removes the claim, carrier and time-path visuals. By that point, those ideas have already been established. The deck closes through pure typography, compressing the full argument into four first-principles statements and then landing the final case for Bitcoin without visual clutter.

# 6. Section 2 — The evolution of money

The active repository contains fifteen history slides. The simplified teaching sequence is:

```text
barter
→ primitive money / collectibles
→ precious metals and gold
→ gold-backed certificates
→ fiat money
→ Bitcoin
```

This is a conceptual model, not a claim that every society followed one perfectly linear path.

## Barter

Barter introduces the double coincidence of wants, difficult standardisation, poor pricing and low scalability. The deeper point is that direct exchange becomes increasingly difficult as specialisation, distance, time and the number of participants increase.

## Collectibles and primitive monetary goods

Collectibles can be recognisable, culturally valued and relatively scarce. Their scarcity may fail when production technology changes or an outside group can create them cheaply. The glass-bead example illustrates that a good may appear scarce locally while being inexpensive to manufacture elsewhere.

The reviewer should examine the wording and historical nuance of this example carefully.

## Precious metals and gold

Global trade makes monetary goods compete. Gold emerges strongly because it is scarce, durable, recognisable, divisible and dense in value. Those same physical characteristics produce frictions in transport, custody, verification and settlement.

## Gold-backed certificates

Certificates improve portability, divisibility and convenience, but separate the bearer instrument from the reserve. This introduces issuer and custodian trust: Is the gold there? Is it fully reserved? Will redemption be honoured? Can claims be issued beyond the reserve?

## Fiat

Fiat removes the binding gold constraint and enables elastic supply, policy discretion, bank credit and efficient digital payments. It also makes monetary integrity depend more heavily on central banks, commercial banks, the state and institutional restraint.

The deck should acknowledge fiat’s genuine usability and liquidity advantages rather than depicting it as technologically useless.

## Bitcoin

Bitcoin introduces digitally native scarcity and bearer-like ownership without a single central issuer. It attempts to combine gold-like scarcity with digital portability, divisibility, global transfer and independent verification.

The precise claim is not that Bitcoin has no governance or dependencies. It is that no single issuer, authority or participant can unilaterally change the supply rules or ownership record.

## Why this section matters

It teaches that:

1. money can take different forms;
2. every form has a rulebook and dependency architecture;
3. monetary evolution is a sequence of trade-offs, not a march from primitive to perfect.

## Review questions

- Is the historical simplification responsible?
- Does the barter section overstate a contested origin story?
- Is the glass-bead example precise?
- Does fiat receive a fair account of its advantages?
- Is Bitcoin presented as a possible monetary evolution rather than an inevitable endpoint?
- Does each transition explain why the next form appeared?

# 7. Section 3 — The functions of money and Bitcoin’s current role

The active repository contains ten slides in this section.

## The three functions

### Medium of exchange
A widely accepted way to settle payment.

### Unit of account
A common denominator in which goods, services, debts and contracts are priced.

### Store of value
A means of carrying purchasing power through time.

These are useful categories, not perfectly isolated real-world boxes.

## Why the functions can separate

An economy may use one currency for transactions, another for saving and another mentally or contractually as a unit of account. The store-of-value function does not disappear merely because the official currency remains dominant for exchange.

## Fiat and long-term saving

Fiat remains highly effective for payments, accounting, settlement and short-term liquidity. Many savers nevertheless do not trust it to preserve purchasing power over long periods, creating demand for property, shares, gold, art, collectibles and other scarce assets.

This mechanism is introduced here and developed fully in Section 4.

## Bitcoin’s current role

The deck positions Bitcoin as:

> **an emerging store of value in an increasingly digital and global economy**

It does not claim Bitcoin is already the dominant medium of exchange or unit of account.

## Stages of monetisation

The intended model is:

```text
collectible
→ store of value
→ medium of exchange
→ unit of account
```

The logic is that broader ownership and liquidity tend to precede widespread exchange and pricing. This is a model, not an iron law, and it must not be used to dismiss present volatility or guarantee future stability.

## The coffee objection

The question “Why can I not buy coffee with Bitcoin everywhere?” is reframed. Payments matter, but judging an emerging monetary asset only by mature medium-of-exchange adoption ignores monetary sequencing.

## Handoff into Section 4

Section 3 ends by positioning Bitcoin between collectible and store of value, then asks what it actually means to store value.

## Review questions

- Are the three functions clear without being too rigid?
- Is monetisation described as a tendency rather than a law?
- Is Bitcoin’s present stage honest?
- Are volatility and adoption uncertainty sufficiently acknowledged?
- Is the transition into Section 4 strong?
- Are any purchasing-power, money-supply or adoption claims stale or insufficiently sourced?

# 8. Section 4 — The Ideal Store of Value

Section 4 is the intellectual and emotional climax. It should feel like a discovery, not a sales sequence.

Its deeper questions are:

1. What is being stored?
2. What does someone receive when earning money?
3. What is saving?
4. What is the difference between a claim and its carrier?
5. What must a carrier preserve?
6. How can it fail?
7. Which requirements follow?
8. How do real assets compare?
9. What monetary demand is Bitcoin competing for?
10. Why do marginal savings flows matter?

## Conceptual foundation

- Value is subjective and not a physical substance.
- Money is introduced as an earned, transferable claim on value.
- The claim is economic, not a legal demand against a named debtor.
- It is not a guaranteed basket or literal percentage ownership of all future production.
- Spending redeems the claim; saving defers redemption.
- The claim is the function; the asset is the carrier.
- A store of value preserves purchasing power and redeemability through time.

## 4.22 — Fixed Supply Reprices at the Margin

```text
Demand does not need to absorb everything.
```

```text
It only needs to grow against a supply that cannot respond.
```

```text
Marginal flows can reprice the entire stock.
```

Only a fraction of the outstanding stock is offered at any moment. Buyers meet sellers at the margin. Most assets can respond to demand through production or issuance; Bitcoin cannot expand through discretionary issuance.

This is not a mechanical price forecast. Liquidity, leverage, expectations, holder behaviour, regulation and macro conditions still matter.

**Visual:** A mathematically aligned rectangular grid represents the fixed outstanding stock. Its unit count and geometry never change. # 14. Intellectual guardrails

## Subjective value

Do not imply that labour time creates objective value or that one hour has a fixed worth. The surgeon provides a context-dependent specialised service.

## Economic claim, not legal debt

The market is not a named legal debtor. The framework is an economic explanation of transferable purchasing-power entitlement.

## No literal share of everything

Do not say the saver legally owns a fixed percentage of every asset or is guaranteed the same basket forever.

## Value is not energy

“Economic energy” may be a secondary metaphor but is not the formal definition. Avoid money literally storing human time or value as a physical substance.

## No perfect Bitcoin

Avoid saying Bitcoin is perfect, risk-free, dependency-free, guaranteed to preserve purchasing power or certain to rise.

## Governance precision

Prefer:

> **No single issuer or authority can unilaterally rewrite the monetary rules.**

Avoid an unqualified “No one controls Bitcoin.”

## Architecture is not valuation

A structurally strong carrier can be expensive; a weaker carrier can be cheap. The table is not an expected-return model.

## Monetary premium is conceptual

Do not assign exact percentages or use full asset market caps as Bitcoin’s automatic total addressable market.

## Marginal repricing is not prediction

Fixed supply explains sensitivity, not a deterministic multiple.

## Future uncertainty

The 100-Year Test improves reasoning under uncertainty; it does not promise Bitcoin’s 2126 purchasing power.

## Gold’s special role

Gold is an established monetary commodity and Bitcoin’s closest traditional store-of-value competitor. It should not be flattened into the same category as a house or operating company.

# 15. Originality and provenance

The old Section 4 drew heavily on a recognisable framework associated with Florian Bruce Boye and *Goldene Zukunft*:

```text
identify a problem
→ repurpose imperfect tools
→ diversify among them
→ engineer a perfect specialised solution
→ most activity migrates to the engineered solution
```

It used lighting, communication and refrigeration examples before applying the pattern to property, shares, gold and Bitcoin. The old ideal-store-of-value thought experiment and parts of the volatility and cash-flow responses also closely resembled that work.

The concern was not that all underlying ideas were exclusive. It was the recognisable combination, sequence, examples and payoff.

The presenter independently created or selected the final ten properties, five assets, comparison framework and scores. Property tables are common in monetary analysis.

Do not reintroduce:

- evolution of problem solving;
- lighting, communication or refrigeration examples;
- repurposed versus purpose-built;
- perfect engineered solution;
- 90% or 99% convergence;
- full-market-cap absorption.

The replacement is:

```text
money as claim
→ claim and carrier
→ saving
→ 100-Year Test
→ inversion
→ failure-derived requirements
→ transparent comparison
→ migration of savings demand
→ monetary premium
→ marginal store-of-value decision
→ fixed-supply repricing
```

# 19. One-paragraph narrative summary

> **The presentation begins by showing that Bitcoin cannot be understood in isolation from money. It traces monetary evolution as a sequence of trade-offs, separates money’s three functions and positions Bitcoin as an emerging store of value. It then rebuilds that thesis from first principles: a surgeon provides a specialised service and receives not the final goods he wants but an earned, transferable claim on value. Spending redeems the claim; saving keeps the exchange open; the claim requires a carrier. A store of value must preserve the claim’s purchasing power and redeemability through time. The 100-Year Test sends the carrier into an unknowable future, inversion identifies ten ways it can fail, and those failures generate ten requirements. Gold, fiat, Bitcoin, real estate and shares are compared transparently. The investment thesis then narrows to monetary demand: when fiat is not trusted over long periods, savings demand migrates into other assets and adds monetary premium. Bitcoin competes for that premium, not for the assets’ underlying productive or consumptive value. The existing stock of wealth need not move at once; every new saving, rebalance, sale, inheritance or refinancing creates another marginal carrier decision. Against a supply that cannot respond through discretionary issuance, those marginal decisions can reprice the stock. Bitcoin therefore does not need to replace everything. It only needs to become the preferred place to store the next unit of value.**

# 20. One-sentence thesis

> **Bitcoin’s investment case is that an asset whose monetary rules cannot be changed unilaterally may win a growing share of humanity’s recurring decision about where to carry deferred value through time.**
